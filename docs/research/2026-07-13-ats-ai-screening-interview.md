## Q1 — AI screening/matching flow in production

### Where the AI score attaches
- **Greenhouse**: "Talent Matching" (Plus/Pro tiers) compares resumes against **weighted calibration criteria** set on the job — i.e., the score is a **job-candidate match**, not a property of the candidate globally. Greenhouse's own support doc (fetched verbatim) states AI features include "Scorecard Summaries" that consolidate *interview* feedback (not CV screening) into "a clear view of agreements, disagreements, and key quotes" — this is a **human-authored scorecard summarized by AI**, not an AI-only score. Source: https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/33043749845403-Greenhouse-AI-features (fetched directly; the article does not explicitly state whether Talent Matching's score attaches to the application row or a separate match entity — this is a genuine gap in what's publicly documented).
- **Ashby**: search aggregate did not surface Ashby's specific CV-screening data model (candidate vs. application-level score) — no primary doc reached. What is clear from aggregated sources: Ashby's **structured feedback/scorecards are mandatory gates** before pipeline advancement — "scorecards aren't optional... required as part of the hiring workflow," and reviewers are blinded from each other's submitted feedback until they submit their own (anti-anchoring pattern also used by Greenhouse). This is an *interview*-stage control, analogous to your `CVScreening` gate concept but applied to human scorecards, not AI CV scores.
- **Workday/HiredScore**: candidate-job match score is explicitly a **job-candidate pairing** — "HiredScore reads requisition context, scores candidates against role requirements" — i.e. one score per (candidate, requisition), matching your `CVScreening` 1:1 on `Application` (which is itself a job×candidate join). This is the industry-standard shape: **the score belongs to the match record, never to the candidate or the job alone**, because a candidate's fit is meaningless without the job context. Your schema already gets this right (`CVScreening.applicationId @unique`, schema.prisma:404).

### Auto-reject vs rank-only
- **Practice**: aggregated industry-commentary sources (not vendor primary docs) report "**about 70% of companies allow AI to reject candidates with zero human oversight**" and that "roughly 83% of Fortune 500 companies are expected to use AI for resume review by end of 2026" — treat this as a **directionally-plausible but unverified secondary claim** (source is an aggregator blog, not a vendor disclosure or a survey I could trace to primary data: https://jobhire.ai/blog/opt-out-ai-resume-screening / https://www.extern.com/post/opt-out-ai-resume-screening-guide). No Greenhouse/Ashby/Workday primary doc I reached explicitly says their own product auto-rejects; all the vendor-side language uses "surface," "recommend," "rank" — consistent with vendors wanting arm's-length distance from an auto-reject decision given the legal exposure below.
- **The documented industry "loophole"**: "AI doesn't have to reject you to bury you — it just has to rank you so low that no human ever scrolls to your position before the role closes. This is legally not a rejection" (same aggregator source — useful as a framing, low authority as a legal claim).
- **Legality**: two separate, real legal threads say auto-reject-without-review is risky:
  - **GDPR Art. 22(1)** (EU/EEA candidates only, but real precedent): "The data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her." The **CJEU SCHUFA ruling (7 Dec 2023)** held that "any candidate scoring tool that materially influences a selection decision falls within Article 22's scope, **even where downstream human review nominally occurs**" if that review isn't genuine — "a human recruiter who clicks 'approve' or 'reject' primarily on the basis of an AI-generated ranking, without independent evaluation of the candidate's file, is not providing the meaningful human review the regulation requires." Sources: https://secureprivacy.ai/blog/gdpr-article-22-automated-decision-making-guide, https://treegarden.io/blog/gdpr-article-22-ai-recruitment-screening/ (a July-2026-dated techtimes.com piece headlined "Automated Hiring Has Broken GDPR Article 22 Since 2018" is a much stronger claim than the underlying regulators' language supports — treat that specific headline as editorializing, not as a regulator statement).
  - **US**: **Mobley v. Workday** (N.D. Cal., filed 2023) — July 12, 2024 ruling allowed claims that Workday acted as an "agent" of its employer-clients to proceed to discovery (i.e., the *AI vendor itself*, not just the employer, can be directly liable under Title VII/ADEA/ADA/FEHA for discriminatory screening); May 16, 2025 preliminary ADEA class certification could sweep in candidates screened since Sept. 2020; a June 2026 order "largely upheld" FEHA/ADA claims. This is the single most concrete legal signal that an AI score with no human-review record is a liability, not just a UX nicety. Sources: https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/mobley-v-workday-court-holds-ai-service-providers-could-be-directly-liable-for-employment-discrimination-under-agent-theory.html, https://hrexecutive.com/judge-refuses-to-dismiss-most-workday-hiring-bias-allegations/, https://www.forbes.com/sites/sheilacallaham/2026/05/29/a-federal-judge-a-1967-law-and-a-billion-rejected-job-applications/
  - **EEOC** (US, Title VII, technical guidance May 18, 2023 — a real primary regulator document, not just commentary): "use of an algorithmic decision-making tool that has an adverse impact will violate Title VII unless the employer can show the use is 'job related and consistent with business necessity'" and critically **"an employer may be liable even if the test was developed or administered by an outside vendor."** The EEOC's FY2024-2028 Strategic Enforcement Plan names "technology-related employment discrimination" (algorithmic decision-making / ML / AI) as an explicit enforcement priority. Sources: https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2023/07/eeoc-issues-title-vii-guidance-on-employer-use-of-ai-other-algorithmic-decisionmaking-tools, https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/eeoc-issues-technical-assistance-guidance-on-the-use-of-advanced-technology-tools-including-artificial-intelligence.html

### Explanation storage
- Metaview's approach is the clearest documented pattern for evidence-linked scoring (interview side, not CV side, but the pattern generalizes): "AI Notes generate the structured summary against the rubric... **tag the evidence to specific moments in the recording** for anyone who wants to verify" and "The AI pulls evidence into the same competency slots the panel will score against." Source: https://www.metaview.ai/interview-notes — the standard is **structured evidence pinned to the specific rubric criterion it supports**, not a single freeform paragraph.
- Greenhouse's Scorecard Attribute Suggestions and Question Suggestions are "generated from job attributes" — i.e., the *rubric itself* is versioned/structured, and AI fills slots in it, rather than free-text output being the primary artifact.
- Your `CVScreening.aiReasoning` (free String) + `matchedSkills`/`missingSkills` (free Json, schema.prisma:413-415) is a simplified version of this pattern — you have evidence (matched/missing skills) but it's unstructured Json with no defined shape, and no evidence-to-CV-location linking (no "this bullet point in the CV supports this match").

### Model/prompt versioning on the score
- No vendor doc I reached publishes *how* they version model/prompt against a stored score (this is treated as an internal ML-ops concern, not a customer-facing spec, across all six vendors' public docs). The best sourced general guidance is from the LLM-ops tooling space (Braintrust, agenta.ai, getmaxim.ai — all vendor blogs for prompt-management products, so read as informed practitioner opinion, not a standard): "**Track prompt versions alongside model versions... in one platform**," "getting started... begins by separating prompts from application code so they can be tracked, tested, and deployed independently," and evaluation "should be run on every prompt change to compare new versions against the current production baseline before promotion." Sources: https://www.braintrust.dev/articles/what-is-prompt-versioning, https://agenta.ai/blog/prompt-versioning-guide, https://www.getmaxim.ai/articles/prompt-versioning-best-practices-for-ai-engineering-teams/
- **Your gap, confirmed by code**: `AiUsageLog` (schema.prisma:442-455) records `modelName`, token counts, duration — this is cost/observability logging, not score provenance. `CVScreening` has no `modelName`/`promptVersion`/`configSnapshot` column, and `AiUsageLog.referenceId` is a loose `String?` with no FK to `CVScreening.screeningId` (schema.prisma:445) — there is no queryable link from a stored score back to which model/prompt/`AiConfig` weights produced it. `AiConfig` (schema.prisma:427-440) has no version number or `effectiveFrom`/`effectiveTo` — if you edit an `AiConfig` row's weights, every historical `CVScreening` that referenced it via `configId` (schema.prisma:406, 422) silently reinterprets under the new weights, because Prisma FK just points at the current row, not a snapshot.

## Q2 — Skills / matching data model

### Industry taxonomy practice
- **LinkedIn Skills Graph** (fetched directly, https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/skills-graph/building-linkedin-s-skills-graph-to-power-a-skills-first-world): "**over 39,000 skills spanning 26 languages**," "**more than 200,000 links between skills**," "**over 374,000 aliases** (different ways to refer to the same skill — e.g., 'data analysis' and 'data analytics')," and a real **hierarchy**, not a flat list: "If a member knows about Artificial Neural Networks, the member knows something about Deep Learning, which means the member knows something about Machine Learning." Curation is hybrid: "curated through a combination of human taxonomists and machine learning" (a KGBert model predicts skill-relationship edges to scale construction). "More than 40% of hirers on LinkedIn explicitly use skills data to fill their roles."
- **ESCO / O\*NET / Lightcast** (aggregated, not fetched primary): "ESCO (EU) is a multilingual taxonomy that maps skills to education and jobs; O\*NET (US) provides deep analysis into occupations and the skills they demand; and Lightcast is a continuously updated library based on job advertisements" — three different curation philosophies (government ontology vs. government occupational survey vs. commercial job-ad-mined). Source: https://www.landing.knovia.ai/blog/skills-taxonomy-esconet-lightcast (vendor blog, moderate authority).
- **Embeddings vs. exact match — real research, arXiv, higher authority than blog aggregation**: CareerBERT and similar systems embed both resume text and ESCO skill/occupation entries into a **shared vector space** and match via cosine similarity; one cited study found "a **cosine similarity threshold of 0.87** provides approximately 90% accuracy for skill matching." Sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02056v1 (CareerBERT), https://arxiv.org/html/2512.03195v1 (ESCO/EQF linking). This is the standard modern approach for the "synonym problem" — not a lookup table, but semantic similarity in embedding space, with taxonomy hierarchy (ESCO/O*NET/LinkedIn) providing the canonical node IDs that embeddings resolve *to*, so two different phrasings collapse onto the same node.
- **Resume-parsing vendors** (Affinda, Textkernel/Sovren, HireAbility, DaXtra — aggregated, no vendor doc fetched directly except a Textkernel developer-docs link surfaced but not read in full): all four are explicitly built around a **taxonomy + synonym-normalization layer as a paid feature**, not a side effect of parsing — "Textkernel... offers semantic search, **skills and job-title normalization**... and built-in skills taxonomies," "Skills are structured in a **Taxonomy > Subtaxonomy > Skill > Child Skill** hierarchy." This confirms: serious vendors treat skill normalization as a first-class subsystem (often licensed/maintained taxonomy content, updated on its own release cadence), not something you bolt onto a parser as free-text output.

### What breaks with the naive approach (yours)
Confirmed against schema: `Skill` (schema.prisma:271-280) is a **flat table**: `skillId`, `name` (globally unique), `category` (free string, no FK to a category table), `synonyms` (Json, no defined shape or index). `JobPostingSkill` (schema.prisma:306-317) is a simple required/optional join with no weight beyond `isRequired`. The AI-written `matchedSkills`/`missingSkills` on `CVScreening` (schema.prisma:414-415) are **free Json, not FKs to `Skill` rows at all** — meaning the AI's notion of "React" in a screening result and the `Skill` table's canonical "React" row are never even guaranteed to be the same string, let alone the same entity.

Concretely, this breaks in the ways the industry pattern above is designed to prevent:
1. **No hierarchy** → "React" and "Frontend Development" can't be related; a candidate who lists "Next.js" but the job requires "React" scores as a total miss even though Next.js implies React (LinkedIn's stated design goal — "if a member knows X, they know something about Y" — is exactly this gap).
2. **`synonyms` Json is unindexed and unenforced** → nothing stops two different `Skill` rows for "JS" and "JavaScript" both existing with divergent synonym lists; there's no canonicalization step, no merge tooling, no uniqueness constraint beyond `name`.
3. **AI free-text `matchedSkills`/`missingSkills` bypass the `Skill` table entirely** → you cannot run "how many candidates match on skill X across all jobs" as a real query; it's a full-text/Json search over unstructured data, not a join.
4. **Exact-string matching (implied by the flat model + no embedding layer) is brittle to real CVs**: "Node" vs "Node.js" vs "NodeJS", "Postgres" vs "PostgreSQL", capitalization, abbreviations. The 0.87-cosine-similarity finding above is one data point on how much a real system needs semantic slack that string equality can't give.

## Q3 — Interview flow: Scorecards/Interview Kits (human) vs. AI interview session model

### Greenhouse / Ashby structured interview data model
- **Greenhouse**: "Every job can be mapped to a defined set of **competencies**, with **interview kits** specifying which competencies each interviewer assesses and scorecard questions standardized across the panel." The workflow is explicitly anti-bias by construction: "feedback forms are structured to collect scoreable data **before interviewers see each other's responses**" (blind submission), then "the debrief workflow aggregates all scores before the hiring manager leads a calibration discussion." So the rollup is: `Competency` (job-level) → `InterviewKit` (assigns competencies to specific interviewer slots in the loop) → `Scorecard` (one per interviewer per interview, filled in by that interviewer, blind to peers) → aggregated at debrief → human decision. Greenhouse's own **Notetaker** feature (2025-2026) now auto-transcribes and "maps AI-generated notes to scorecard questions" — i.e. AI assists filling the human's scorecard, doesn't replace the human's score. Source: https://support.greenhouse.io/hc/en-us/articles/33043749845403-Greenhouse-AI-features (fetched) + aggregated search results.
- **Ashby**: same shape — "scorecards aren't optional... required as part of the hiring workflow" (gating), "controls in place to ensure interviewers can only see feedback once they've submitted their own" (same blind pattern as Greenhouse). Scorecards "feed into Ashby's reporting... track quality-of-hire tied to structured scorecard data."
- **Your gap, confirmed by code**: `InterviewSchedule` (schema.prisma:527-545) has **no scorecard/feedback entity at all** — fields are only `interviewType`, `scheduledDate/Time`, `onlineMeetingLink`, `status: scheduled|completed|cancelled`. A completed human interview literally records nothing but a status flip to `completed` — there is no competency, no rubric, no per-interviewer feedback, no rollup, no way a hiring decision could ever cite what happened in that interview. This is the single largest structural gap versus every mainstream ATS in this research (Greenhouse, Ashby, and by extension SmartRecruiters/Lever/etc. all treat structured scorecards as *the* core interview data model, not an afterthought).

### AI mock/screening interview session model (HireVue, Karat, Metaview)
- **Karat** (search aggregate, no primary doc fetched): sessions are structured as `intro/discussion (~10min) → live coding (~40min)`, scored via a **fixed weighted rubric** ("problem-solving approach, communication while solving, code correctness, code clarity, ability to test and debug") filled in by a human "Interview Engineer" during/after the loop, then packaged as "the video, the coding pad, the rubric notes, and the question prompts into a single playable session." Karat reports "**98.8% recommendation consistency**" across interviewers scoring the same skill level — i.e. they explicitly QC for scorer agreement, a practice with no analog in your system (no inter-rater or inter-run consistency check on Gemini's scoring).
- **HireVue** — session resumption is explicitly documented for candidates: "If you are disconnected between answering questions, you can simply **resume your interview**, and you will go to the next question **while your previous answers are safely saved**"; "if disconnected while answering a question, the question has most likely been submitted, and when you re-enter the interview, you will go to the next question." There is also a defined escalation path: "finish the interview and then contact the company... they can **send a retake** if needed." Source: https://hirevuesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028433551-Disconnected-During-the-Interview. This is a directly comparable feature to your `InterviewSession.status` enum — HireVue's pattern is: **per-answer durability + automatic resume to the next unanswered question + an explicit retake/reset path**, none of which your system implements (see gap table).
- **Metaview** models the AI artifact as tightly coupled to a pre-existing human rubric: "**structured artifact mapped to your scorecard**... TLDR, Q&A, motivations, and open questions surfaced automatically," "**tag the evidence to specific moments in the recording**." The AI never invents its own scoring scheme — it fills a scorecard schema a human defined for that role/competency.
- **HireVue proctoring boundary** (a real nuance): "HireVue's proctoring add-on covers identity verification and behavioral analysis during recorded video responses, but it is interview proctoring, **not coding environment proctoring**, and cannot monitor a candidate writing actual code" — i.e. even a mature commercial vendor scopes what it can reliably attest to; not directly load-bearing for your capstone but useful context if your thesis defense gets a "how do you prevent cheating" question — the honest answer, per this evidence, is "commercial vendors don't fully solve this either."

## Q4 — Interview scheduling

Aggregated search only (no interview-scheduling-vendor primary doc fetched — ModernLoop, Metaview, treegarden.io, Cal.com blog posts synthesized by the search tool; read as informed practitioner descriptions, not vendor API specs):

- **Double-booking prevention** is achieved by making the calendar write **atomic with the availability read**: "the ATS creates the calendar event immediately — reducing the window between availability check and event creation to milliseconds rather than minutes, and a slot that was available at the time of the recruiter's availability check is **locked by the calendar event** before any competing commitment can be added to the same window." I.e. real systems don't just "check then insert" against their own DB — they push the hold into the external calendar (Google/Outlook) as the source of truth for availability, because the interviewer's calendar can also be booked from outside the ATS.
- **Buffers**: "if an interviewer has a meeting ending at 2pm and needs 15 minutes between meetings, that buffer can be configured into the availability calculation."
- **Panel scheduling**: "collective scheduling for panels, multi-stage loop support, and **round-robin distribution** — ideally weighted, so part-time or busier interviewers aren't overloaded" — i.e. panel = multiple interviewer rows against one time slot, with a load-balancing assignment algorithm, not manual single-`interviewerId` assignment.
- **Timezone**: "detects and adjusts for time zone differences automatically, so candidates and interviewers always see the correct local time."
- **Meeting-link generation**: "the system automatically checks all participants' availability, creates a Google Calendar event... and **generates a Google Meet link** if the interview is remote" — link generation is a side effect of the calendar-API call, not a manually pasted string.
- No source directly addressed **reschedule history** as an explicit audit trail feature; reasonable inference (not verified) is that most ATSs represent a reschedule as a mutation of the same calendar event plus an activity-log entry, analogous to your `ApplicationHistory` pattern already used elsewhere in this schema (schema.prisma:381-396) — you already have the *pattern* for this, just not applied to `InterviewSchedule`.

### Your gap, confirmed by code
`InterviewSchedule` (schema.prisma:527-545): single `interviewerId` (one interviewer, no panel), `scheduledDate`/`scheduledTime` as separate `DateTime` columns with no explicit timezone field or UTC-normalization documented in-schema, `onlineMeetingLink String?` (free text — no calendar-API integration, so nothing is actually checking the interviewer's calendar before this row is written), and **no availability/double-booking check possible** because there's no interviewer-availability model at all — the only thing preventing two `InterviewSchedule` rows for the same interviewer at overlapping times is... nothing; there's no unique constraint or overlap check in the schema, and none of the standard mitigations (calendar-API hold, buffer config, panel rows) exist.

## Q5 — Compliance & audit guardrails

### NYC Local Law 144 (AEDT bias audit) — concrete, dated, real regulator source
- Effective **July 5, 2023** (enacted), enforcement began after amendments. Requires: (1) an **independent bias audit within one year of use** of any Automated Employment Decision Tool, (2) a summary of results **published publicly** ("date of the most recent bias audit, a summary of results, and distribution date of the AEDT prior to its use"), (3) **10 business days' advance notice** to any NYC-resident candidate/employee before the tool evaluates them, including "instructions for how to request an alternative selection process or a reasonable accommodation" and disclosure of "the specific job qualifications and characteristics the tool will evaluate." Penalties: "$500 for the initial offense," "$500 to $1,500 for each additional violation" — **per-candidate**, i.e. "each candidate who does not receive the required notice counts as a separate violation." Source: https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/about/automated-employment-decision-tools.page (official NYC DCWP page, cited via aggregator but this is the correct primary regulator page), corroborated by https://legalclarity.org/nyc-local-law-144-bias-audit-requirements-and-penalties/. HireVue's own compliance response is directly documented: engaged DCI Consulting Group in Jan 2023, producing "**nearly 300 different bias audit tables**... across HireVue's library of competencies for national and New York City-specific results... across several occupational groupings" — source: https://www.hirevue.com/press-release/hirevue-leads-industry-in-fair-and-ethical-hiring-practice-engaging-external-auditor-dci-consulting-group-for-external-bias-audit-of-algorithms

### EU AI Act — high-risk classification for employment AI
- Recruitment/selection AI (CV filtering, candidate evaluation, targeted job ads) is explicitly named as **high-risk**: "AI tools will generally be classified as high-risk where they materially influence access to employment opportunities, **even if a human recruiter makes the final decision**" — i.e. the "we just rank, a human decides" defense does not exempt you from high-risk classification under the Act's own framing.
- **Timeline moved**: originally Aug 2, 2026 for high-risk obligations; per the "Digital Omnibus" agreement this has been **postponed to Dec 2, 2027**. On May 19, 2026 the European Commission published **draft guidelines on Article 6 high-risk classification** — still not finalized as of this snapshot. Treat any specific compliance date here as **actively moving** — this is exactly the kind of fact this brief's freshness contract exists for. Sources: https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/2026/The-Digital-AI-Omnibus-Proposed-deferral-of-high-risk-AI-obligations-under-the-AI-Act, https://www.warden-ai.com/resources/eu-ai-act-hiring-recruitment
- High-risk obligations (once binding) include: "risk management, data governance and management, **technical documentation, record-keeping**, transparency of operations, instructions for deployers, **human oversight**, accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity" — this is a direct list of what a data model must support (an audit trail table, a documented model-version record, a human-oversight/override record).
- Not directly relevant to a Vietnamese student capstone's actual deployment jurisdiction, but relevant as **the reference standard for "what good looks like"** in a thesis defense — you can cite it as the target shape without claiming EU jurisdiction applies to you.

### GDPR Article 22 (only binds if you process EU/EEA data subjects — flag this scoping honestly)
- Already covered under Q1. Restated for the data-model angle: what Art. 22 compliance forces into a data model, per the sources above, is a **human-review record that is distinguishable from a rubber stamp** — i.e., not just a `reviewedBy` column, but evidence the reviewer saw the case-specific reasoning and could have overridden it (timestamp of review vs. timestamp of AI score, a free-text override/agree field, ideally a record of what the human saw).

### EEOC (US, Title VII — real primary source, May 18, 2023 technical assistance document, still current per Strategic Enforcement Plan FY2024-2028)
- Core rule: an AI selection tool causing **adverse (disparate) impact** violates Title VII "unless the employer can show the use is job related and consistent with business necessity" — the classic **four-fifths rule** framing from pre-AI EEOC guidance still applies to AI tools.
- Vendor pass-through liability is explicit: "an employer may be liable **even if the test was developed or administered by an outside vendor**" — directly relevant if this system is ever pitched as a real product rather than a capstone: you (the vendor) don't get to point at Gemini as the liable party, and neither does whichever employer might deploy this.
- Concretely what this forces into a data model to be *auditable* (not necessarily to be *legal* — that's a policy/process question no schema alone solves): you need enough historical data to compute adverse-impact statistics by protected class **without collecting protected-class data in the applicant-facing tables** — i.e., a separate, access-restricted table joined only for audit computation, not exposed in the normal application/screening read path. Nothing like this exists in your schema, and — correctly, for a capstone — building it is almost certainly out of scope (see recommendations).

### Concrete data-model requirements compliance actually forces (synthesized across all four regimes above)
1. **An audit-log table for AI decisions** distinct from `AiUsageLog` (which is cost/perf telemetry) — one row per *decision event* (score computed, recommendation made, human override), not one row per API call.
2. **A human-review/override record** on any AI output that gates a real hiring action — who reviewed, when, what they saw, whether they agreed or overrode, timestamped *after* the AI score so it's provably not simultaneous/rubber-stamp.
3. **Model/prompt/config version captured on the decision row itself** (not just in a separate mutable `AiConfig` row that can silently change meaning retroactively — see Q1's `AiConfig` versioning gap).
4. **Consent + notice tracking** if any jurisdiction requiring advance notice (NYC LL144's 10-business-day rule is the concrete example) applies — a `notifiedAt` timestamp per candidate per tool-use, minimum.
5. **Retention policy fields** (not found as a concrete requirement in any single source above, but implied by GDPR data-minimization generally) — none of these regimes was cited giving a specific retention *duration* for AI hiring records; treat this as an open question rather than a sourced fact.

## Q6 — Reliability patterns for AI pipelines (general LLM-ops practice, not ATS-specific)

Sourced from LLM-ops tooling vendor blogs (Braintrust, agenta.ai, getmaxim.ai, mightybot.ai, tianpan.co) — read as informed current practitioner consensus, not a formal standard body:

- **Retry**: "exponential backoff **with jitter** and per-error-type configuration" — rate-limit errors get backoff; malformed-request errors need reformulation, not blind retry ("different errors require different strategies"). Check provider `Retry-After`/`x-ratelimit-reset-tokens` headers before computing your own backoff.
- **Idempotency**: "traditional idempotency breaks when outputs are stochastic" — for LLM calls, idempotency is about **not double-charging/double-writing on retry**, not about getting the same output twice. Practical mechanism: an idempotency key on the *job*, checked before re-submitting to the provider, so a retried BullMQ job doesn't re-call Gemini and re-write a second `CVScreening`/`AiUsageLog` row for work already done.
- **Dead-letter queue**: "if a task fails after all retries, the payload metadata is sent to a DLQ for manual inspection, preventing the failure from crashing the entire orchestration server" — this is exactly BullMQ's built-in failed-job list; the missing piece per your own audit finding is that your processor **swallows errors**, so BullMQ's retry/DLQ mechanism never even engages (a caught-and-logged exception looks like a completed job to BullMQ).
- **Prompt/model versioning + eval**: "separating prompts from application code so they can be tracked, tested, and deployed independently," "automated evaluations should be run on every prompt change to compare new versions against the current production baseline before promotion." For parse/score quality specifically, the standard is a **held-out labeled eval set** (a small number of CVs with human-verified expected parse output / expected score) re-run whenever the prompt or model changes, not just "it looked right in a manual test."
- **Cost/token tracking**: your `AiUsageLog` (schema.prisma:442-455) already implements this correctly — `promptTokens`, `completionTokens`, `durationMs`, `status` per call is exactly the standard shape.

## Ranked recommendations for a capstone

Ordered by (impressiveness in a thesis defense × how cheaply it closes) — highest first. Explicitly separated from "acceptable to leave open."

### Close these (cheap, and a thesis committee will ask about them)
1. **Stop swallowing errors in the screening processor; let BullMQ's retry/DLQ actually engage.** This is your own audit's most damning finding (retryCount written, never read; errors swallowed). It's a few-line fix in the processor and directly demonstrates you understand the "mocks don't catch integration bugs" lesson this environment's own verification rule exists for. Cite the "different errors need different strategies" pattern (Q6) if asked why you're not just blindly retrying everything.
2. **Enforce `minimumScoreThreshold`, or explicitly decide not to and document why (human-in-the-loop by design, not by accident).** Given GDPR Art.22/Mobley v. Workday findings above, the *defensible* answer in a thesis defense is: "the AI recommendation surfaces and ranks; a human recruiter must act on `aiRecommendation` before an `ApplicationHistory` transition to `rejected` is allowed — auto-reject was deliberately not implemented, matching the industry's own legal exposure concerns," not "we forgot to enforce it." Wiring `aiRecommendation`/`overallScore` into the recruiter-facing UI as an actionable, sortable field (not just a stored value) closes most of the practical gap cheaply, without needing an actual auto-reject.
3. **Add a minimal human-review record on `CVScreening`** — e.g. `reviewedBy`, `reviewedAt`, `humanDecision`. This single addition is what separates "we have an AI score" from "we have a defensible human-in-the-loop system," and it's a one-migration change. Directly answers the GDPR Art.22 "meaningful human review" concern (Q1/Q5) without needing the full EU AI Act documentation apparatus.
4. **Add a timeout/reaper for `InterviewSession` and actually use the `abandon` status.** A BullMQ delayed job or a scheduled sweep that flips stale `in_progress` sessions to `abandon` after N minutes of inactivity is cheap and directly closes a gap your own audit already named as dead code (the `abandon` enum value with no writer).
5. **Snapshot model/prompt/config identity onto the decision row.** Add `modelName`/`promptVersion` (or a `configSnapshot` Json) directly on `CVScreening`/`InterviewResult`, and/or add an `AiUsageLog.screeningId`/`sessionId` FK so a stored score is traceable to exactly what produced it, even after `AiConfig` weights change later. Small schema change, addresses a real gap this brief's sources call out repeatedly (LLM-ops consensus, EU AI Act "record-keeping").

### Worth doing if time allows (moderate effort, strong "we understood the domain" signal)
6. **A minimal `InterviewFeedback`/scorecard entity for `InterviewSchedule`** — even a bare `{ interviewId, submittedBy, rating, notes, recommendation }` closes the single largest structural gap found in this research (Greenhouse/Ashby's entire interview data model exists around this). Doesn't need blind-submission or multi-interviewer panels to be a meaningful improvement over "a completed interview records nothing."
7. **FK `matchedSkills`/`missingSkills` to real `Skill` rows** instead of free Json, even without embeddings — this alone (exact-match against the canonical `Skill.name`, using the existing `synonyms` Json as an alias lookup before falling back to Gemini's free text) meaningfully closes the "AI's skill names and the DB's skill names are different universes" gap at low cost, and is a natural talking point next to the LinkedIn/ESCO taxonomy research above even without embeddings.

### Acceptable to leave open (name these explicitly as scoped-out, don't apologize for them)
8. **Full skills-taxonomy hierarchy + embedding-based semantic matching** (LinkedIn/ESCO/CareerBERT-style). This is a genuinely large, separate research project (the cited papers are themselves publishable NLP work) — reasonable to cite as "future work, Phase 2 RAG-adjacent" (your own roadmap already flags `pgvector`/RAG for Phase 2) rather than something a capstone needs to ship.
9. **Real calendar-API integration for scheduling** (Google/Outlook availability holds, panel round-robin, auto Zoom/Meet link generation). This requires OAuth app registration, calendar webhook handling, and a genuinely different scheduling architecture — reasonably out of scope. If you want a cheap partial credit item: add a DB-level overlap check (`WHERE interviewerId = ? AND scheduledDate/Time overlaps`) before insert — that's a few lines and at least demonstrates awareness of the double-booking problem without building calendar integration.
10. **NYC LL144-style independent bias audit, EU AI Act technical documentation dossier, EEOC adverse-impact statistical monitoring.** These require real candidate volume, protected-class data collection under separate governance, and (for LL144) an actual independent third-party auditor — categorically not achievable or even meaningful for a capstone with synthetic/small data. Name the requirement in your thesis as "identified but explicitly out of scope, and here's why" (no real candidate population, no protected-class data collected) rather than attempting a token implementation that would misrepresent what real compliance requires.
11. **Session-level cheating/proctoring controls for the AI mock interview.** Even HireVue's own proctoring is scoped ("interview proctoring, not coding environment proctoring" — Q3) — a capstone shouldn't be expected to solve what commercial vendors themselves don't fully solve.

**Open questions for the main agent to resolve before acting on any of the above:**
- Is there any intent to demo this system with real (even if small) candidate data that would make Q5's compliance items partially relevant, or is it purely synthetic/mock data for the defense? This changes whether items 10-11 are "acceptable to skip" or "must at least be named as a known limitation in the written thesis."
- For recommendation #2 (enforce vs. don't enforce threshold): confirm whether the roadmap's Phase 0/Phase 1 split has an opinion already — `docs/migration-roadmap.md` should be checked before committing to "surface but don't auto-act" as the fix, in case Phase 1 already scopes this differently.
